What is iron?

Iron is an essential mineral your body uses to transport oxygen, produce energy, and keep cells functioning properly.2 In food and supplements, iron generally comes in one of two forms:24

Heme iron comes primarily from animal foods like red meat, poultry, and seafood. It’s generally absorbed more efficiently and is less affected by other foods eaten at the same meal.

Non-heme iron is found in plant foods, fortified foods, and most iron supplements. Its absorption can vary more depending on diet, medications, and other nutrients. For example, vitamin C can improve non-heme iron absorption, while compounds in tea, coffee, calcium supplements, and some grains may reduce it.

Once absorbed, most of the iron in your body is found inside red blood cells, where it becomes part of a protein called hemoglobin. Hemoglobin picks up oxygen in the lungs and delivers it throughout the body.

Without enough iron, your body can’t produce healthy red blood cells efficiently, and tissues may not get the oxygen they need. This can eventually lead to iron-deficiency anemia — a condition in which the blood cannot carry oxygen as effectively as it should.11

That’s one reason low iron levels can leave people feeling tired, weak, cold, dizzy, or short of breath.12 Some people also experience headaches, reduced exercise tolerance, brain fog, brittle nails, hair shedding, or restless leg symptoms. In some cases, the effects of low iron on energy levels and concentration can appear even before anemia fully develops.12

Iron’s role in the body goes far beyond oxygen transport, though. It’s also involved in:

Energy production

Iron helps enzymes inside your mitochondria — the “power plants” of your cells — convert nutrients into ATP, the body’s primary energy source.14 Low iron levels can reduce energy production efficiency, which may contribute to fatigue and reduced stamina.

Muscle function

Iron helps muscles use and store oxygen through a protein called myoglobin.15 This is one reason athletes — especially endurance athletes — often monitor iron status closely.

Brain function

Iron helps your body produce neurotransmitters involved in attention, memory, and mood.16 17 Research has linked low iron status to impaired concentration, reduced cognitive performance, and mental fatigue — particularly in women and adolescents.18

Immune health

Immune cells rely on iron to grow and respond appropriately to infections.19 Interestingly, both too little and too much iron can disrupt immune function, which is one reason the body regulates iron carefully.20

Pregnancy and fetal development

Iron needs increase significantly during pregnancy because the body must support a growing fetus, placenta, and expanded maternal blood volume.21 Adequate iron intake helps support healthy fetal development and reduces the risk of maternal iron-deficiency anemia.

Cell growth and repair

Iron is involved in DNA synthesis and cellular repair processes throughout the body.22 Rapidly growing tissues — including those involved in pregnancy, childhood, and athletic recovery — tend to have higher iron demands.

Your body tightly controls iron absorption because, unlike with some nutrients, it has no efficient way to actively remove excess iron.23 That means balance matters: too little iron can impair oxygen delivery and energy production, while too much iron may damage tissues through oxidative stress.

Different types of iron supplements

Iron supplements come in several different forms, and the type you choose can affect absorption, elemental iron content, and the likelihood of side effects like constipation or nausea.25

Part of this comes down to chemistry. Iron is a reactive mineral, so supplement companies bind it to different compounds to improve stability, absorption, or tolerability. Some forms release iron quickly in the digestive tract, which may improve absorption but also irritate the stomach and intestines.25 Others release iron more slowly or bind it to amino acids, which may reduce gastrointestinal side effects.

Here’s how the most common types compare:

Ferrous sulfate

Ferrous sulfate is the most commonly used and widely studied iron supplement.26 It’s effective and inexpensive, which is why it’s often considered the standard treatment for iron deficiency and iron-deficiency anemia. However, it’s also one of the forms most commonly associated with constipation, nausea, stomach pain, and dark stools.25

Chemically, sulfate releases iron relatively readily in the digestive tract. That can support absorption, but it also means more free iron may interact with the lining of the stomach and intestines, contributing to irritation and GI symptoms.

Ferrous bisglycinate (chelated iron)

Ferrous bisglycinate binds iron to the amino acid glycine.27 This “chelated” structure helps shield the iron as it moves through the digestive tract, which may explain why studies often find fewer gastrointestinal side effects compared to ferrous sulfate.3 4 It’s commonly used in supplements designed for people with sensitive stomachs.

Ferrous fumarate

Ferrous fumarate contains a relatively high amount of elemental iron per dose.28 It’s commonly found in prescription iron products and over-the-counter supplements, though it can still cause digestive side effects similar to ferrous sulfate in some people.

Ferrous gluconate

Ferrous gluconate contains less elemental iron per tablet than sulfate or fumarate, but some people find it easier to tolerate.29 30 Because each tablet contains less iron overall, larger or more frequent doses may sometimes be needed.

Carbonyl iron

Carbonyl iron consists of purified elemental iron particles rather than iron attached to a salt.25 The particles dissolve and absorb more slowly, which may reduce stomach irritation and lower toxicity risk.31 However, it can also take longer to replenish iron stores.

Heme iron polypeptide

Heme iron comes from animal sources and is generally absorbed more efficiently than non-heme iron.24 Unlike most supplemental iron, it enters the body through a separate absorption pathway that’s less affected by food or stomach conditions.25 Heme iron supplements may work well for people who struggle to absorb traditional iron salts, though they tend to cost more and are not vegetarian-friendly.

Why do women need more iron than men?

Every time blood is lost, iron is lost with it. For some women, especially those with heavy menstrual bleeding, that monthly loss can add up quickly.2 Over time, iron stores may gradually decline, eventually leading to iron deficiency or iron-deficiency anemia if intake and absorption don’t keep pace.

Pregnancy increases iron demands even further. During pregnancy, the body needs additional iron to support:21 32

Expanded blood volume

A pregnant woman’s blood volume increases significantly during pregnancy. More blood means a greater need for hemoglobin and, therefore, more iron.

Fetal growth and development

Hence, iron deficiency is especially common during pregnancy. Low iron levels during this time have been associated with fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, increased risk of maternal anemia, preterm birth, and low birth weight.33

Women may also be at higher risk of low iron status for several other reasons. Restrictive diets, endurance exercise, gastrointestinal disorders, frequent blood donation, and certain medications can all affect iron intake, absorption, or losses.24 Women who avoid red meat or follow vegetarian or vegan diets may also need to pay closer attention to iron intake, since plant-based non-heme iron is generally absorbed less efficiently than heme iron from animal foods.

Importantly, not all fatigue in women is caused by iron deficiency. Symptoms like tiredness, hair shedding, or brain fog can have many potential causes. That’s one reason many experts recommend confirming iron deficiency through lab testing — often including ferritin and hemoglobin levels — before starting high-dose iron supplementation over the long term.34

Iron dosage for women

Iron dosage depends on factors like age, diet, iron status, pregnancy, and whether someone is trying to prevent deficiency or correct an existing one. The amount listed on a supplement label usually refers to elemental iron — the actual amount of usable iron the body receives.

For women, the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for iron is:2

Women ages 19-50: 18mg daily

Women 51+: 8mg daily

Pregnancy: 27mg daily

Breastfeeding: 9-10mg daily

Iron supplements used to treat deficiency or iron-deficiency anemia often contain much higher amounts than the daily recommended intake.28 Common over-the-counter products may provide anywhere from 18mg to 65mg of elemental iron per serving, while some prescription regimens use even higher doses under medical supervision.

Iron absorption can also vary depending on how the supplement is taken. Many experts recommend taking iron on an empty stomach with vitamin C or a vitamin C-containing beverage to improve absorption.35 At the same time, calcium supplements, dairy products, tea, coffee, and some medications may reduce absorption if taken too closely together with an iron supplement.

Alternate-day iron dosing may improve absorption

For years, many people were told to take iron supplements once or even multiple times daily. But newer research suggests that taking iron every other day may improve absorption in some cases while also reducing gastrointestinal side effects.

Researchers became interested in alternate-day dosing after studies found that oral iron supplements temporarily increase hepcidin, a hormone that helps regulate iron absorption.36 After taking an iron supplement, hepcidin levels can remain elevated for about 24 hours, temporarily reducing how much additional iron the body absorbs during that time.37

In a 2020 study published in Haematologica, iron-deficient women absorbed significantly more iron when supplements were taken on alternate days instead of consecutive days.38 A later randomized controlled trial published in The Lancet found that alternate-day dosing also produced fewer gastrointestinal side effects while maintaining similar ferritin improvements over time.39

That doesn’t mean everyone should take iron only every other day. People with severe anemia or very low iron levels may still require more aggressive supplementation under medical supervision. But for women struggling with nausea, constipation, or stomach discomfort from traditional iron regimens, alternate-day dosing may be worth discussing with a healthcare provider.

Are iron supplements safe?

Iron supplements are generally safe for most healthy adults when used appropriately, but side effects and excessive intake can occur — especially at higher doses.2

Common side effects

Constipation, nausea, stomach discomfort, bloating, and dark stools are the most frequently reported side effects of oral iron supplements. Taking iron with food may reduce stomach irritation, though it can also decrease absorption somewhat.

Medication interactions

Iron can interfere with the absorption of certain medications, including thyroid hormone replacement, some antibiotics, and osteoporosis medications.24 Calcium supplements may also reduce iron absorption if taken too closely together.

Iron overload

Unlike some vitamins, the body has no efficient way to actively eliminate excess iron.24 Taking high-dose iron long-term without medical need may eventually contribute to iron overload and oxidative stress.

Toxicity risk in children

An accidental iron overdose can be extremely dangerous in young children. Iron-containing supplements should always be stored securely and kept out of reach.

Certain medical conditions

People with conditions involving excess iron storage — such as hereditary hemochromatosis — should not take iron supplements unless specifically instructed by a healthcare provider.24 People with chronic liver disease or repeated blood transfusions may also require additional caution.

Because symptoms like fatigue, hair shedding, and low energy can have many possible causes, it’s often a good idea to confirm iron deficiency with lab testing before starting long-term high-dose supplementation.12 Ferritin, hemoglobin, and other iron-related blood tests can help determine whether supplemental iron is actually needed.

Who are iron supplements for?

Iron supplements are most useful for women who lack sufficient iron because of diet, absorption, or normal physiological demands like menstruation and pregnancy.2 In some cases, supplementation may help restore iron stores, improve iron-deficiency anemia, and reduce symptoms like fatigue, poor exercise tolerance, or brain fog related to low iron status.11 13

More specifically, the following groups stand to benefit most from supplementation:

Women with heavy menstrual bleeding

Menstruation is one of the primary reasons women require more iron than men.2 Women with heavy periods can lose substantial amounts of iron month after month, increasing the risk of low ferritin and iron-deficiency anemia over time.34

Pregnant women

Iron requirements increase significantly during pregnancy because the body must support expanded blood volume, placental growth, and fetal development.21 For this reason, iron is included in most prenatal supplements, and many pregnancy guidelines recommend routine iron supplementation.33

Women with confirmed iron deficiency or low ferritin

Women with low ferritin, low hemoglobin, or diagnosed iron-deficiency anemia are among the clearest candidates for iron supplementation.11 Depending on severity, supplementation may help improve fatigue, exercise tolerance, cognitive function, and overall quality of life over time.13 17 18

Vegetarians and vegans

Plant-based diets contain only non-heme iron, which is generally absorbed less efficiently than heme iron from animal foods.24 Women following vegetarian or vegan diets may benefit from paying closer attention to iron intake and, in some cases, supplementation.

Endurance athletes and highly active women

Iron plays a major role in oxygen transport and muscle function.15 Endurance athletes — especially runners and female athletes — may face increased iron demands through training stress, sweating, gastrointestinal microbleeding, and foot-strike hemolysis (the breakdown of red blood cells during repetitive impact exercise).

Women with gastrointestinal absorption issues

Certain gastrointestinal conditions can impair iron absorption, including celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, prior gastric bypass surgery, and chronic acid-suppressing medication use.29 Some women in these groups may require supplementation even with adequate dietary intake.

Iron supplementation is often most effective when combined with identifying the underlying reason iron levels became low in the first place. In some cases, that may involve diet changes, managing heavy menstrual bleeding, addressing gastrointestinal conditions, or adjusting medications that interfere with absorption.

Who are they not for?

Iron supplements can be extremely helpful when iron levels are genuinely low. But more iron is not always better, and supplementation isn’t appropriate for everyone.2

Many symptoms associated with low iron — including fatigue, hair shedding, brain fog, and poor exercise tolerance — can also stem from unrelated issues like poor sleep, thyroid disorders, chronic stress, depression, or other nutrient deficiencies.34 That’s one reason iron supplements tend to work best when low ferritin, low hemoglobin, or iron deficiency has actually been confirmed.

The following groups may want to reconsider supplementation or exercise additional caution before taking an iron supplement:

People with normal iron levels

Iron is not a stimulant or general “energy booster.” If iron levels are already normal, taking additional iron is unlikely to improve energy and may eventually contribute to excess iron accumulation over time.24

People assuming fatigue automatically means iron deficiency

Fatigue is one of the most common symptoms of iron deficiency, but it’s also one of the most nonspecific.34 Symptoms like low energy, dizziness, poor concentration, and hair shedding can have many possible causes beyond iron status.

People with hereditary hemochromatosis or elevated iron stores

Some people absorb and store too much iron naturally due to genetic conditions like hereditary hemochromatosis.24 In these cases, additional iron supplementation may increase the risk of iron overload and tissue damage.

People expecting immediate results

Iron supplementation usually works gradually, not overnight. Depending on how depleted iron stores are, noticeable improvements in energy or symptoms may take several weeks or even months.11

People already taking iron-containing multivitamins or prenatals

Some women unintentionally “stack” iron supplements on top of iron-containing multivitamins, prenatals, or fortified products.2 That can push total intake much higher than intended, especially when using multiple supplements daily.

People who can’t tolerate iron supplements despite adjustments

Not all iron supplements behave the same way in the digestive tract.25 Someone who experienced constipation or nausea with ferrous sulfate may tolerate a different form — such as ferrous bisglycinate — or an alternate-day dosing schedule more comfortably.3 39

However, people who consistently cannot tolerate iron supplements — even after changing forms or dosing schedules — should speak with a healthcare provider before continuing supplementation. Some people also use lactoferrin-based supplements as an alternative approach to iron support.40

In summary, iron supplements tend to work best when they’re targeted toward an actual deficiency, matched to the individual’s tolerance and needs, and used consistently over time — rather than treated as a catch-all solution for low energy or wellness.

Alternatives to iron supplements

Iron supplements can be useful for women with increased needs, low iron intake, or confirmed deficiency, but they aren’t the only approach to supporting healthy iron levels. In many cases, improving dietary intake or supporting iron absorption may be enough — particularly for women with mild shortfalls rather than significant anemia.

Diet

For most people, food remains the best long-term foundation for maintaining healthy iron levels.24 Iron is naturally present in many foods and is also added to certain fortified products like breakfast cereals and breads.54

Some of the most common dietary iron sources include:

Lean meat, seafood, and poultry

Iron-fortified cereals and breads

Lentils, beans, peas, and spinach

Nuts and certain dried fruits like raisins

Iron in food exists in two forms: heme iron and non-heme iron.54 Heme iron — found in meat, seafood, and poultry — is generally absorbed more efficiently. Non-heme iron is found primarily in plant foods and fortified products.

For women with mild deficiency or borderline intake, consistently eating more iron-rich foods may be enough to improve iron status over time, particularly when combined with strategies that support absorption.

Not every supplement aimed at iron health directly provides large amounts of elemental iron. Some instead focus on improving absorption or supporting iron regulation in different ways.

Vitamin C is one of the best-known examples. It helps improve the absorption of non-heme iron by helping maintain iron in a more absorbable form within the digestive tract.5 That’s why many iron supplements pair iron with vitamin C directly. Lactoferrin

Found naturally in milk and colostrum, lactoferrin is an iron-binding protein that appears to support iron regulation and absorption rather than directly supplying large amounts of elemental iron.40 Some research suggests lactoferrin supplementation may improve iron markers while causing fewer gastrointestinal side effects than traditional iron salts like ferrous sulfate. However, the evidence remains smaller and less standardized overall, so lactoferrin is probably better viewed as a complementary or alternative approach rather than a direct replacement for standard iron therapy in women with significant deficiency or anemia.

Iron supplement FAQ

What is the best form of iron to take?

There isn’t one universally “best” form of iron — it depends on your goals and how well your stomach tolerates it. Ferrous sulfate is one of the most studied and commonly prescribed forms for correcting iron deficiency, but it also causes gastrointestinal side effects more often.8 25

Forms like ferrous bisglycinate or iron glycinate are often easier to tolerate while still improving iron markers effectively.3 4 That’s one reason many of our top picks in this guide use bisglycinate forms rather than traditional ferrous sulfate.

When is the best time to take iron supplements?

Iron is generally absorbed best on an empty stomach, particularly when taken alongside vitamin C or a vitamin C-containing beverage.35 However, taking iron without food can also increase the likelihood of nausea or stomach discomfort for some people.

If iron upsets your stomach, taking it with a small amount of food is often a reasonable compromise. Many experts also recommend avoiding calcium supplements, dairy, tea, and coffee close to your iron dose because they can interfere with absorption.24

Should I take iron every day?

Not necessarily. Newer research suggests that alternate-day dosing may actually improve iron absorption in some women while also reducing gastrointestinal side effects.38 39

That’s because iron supplements temporarily increase hepcidin, a hormone that regulates iron absorption.36 When hepcidin stays elevated, the body may absorb less iron from closely spaced doses. For some women — especially those struggling with constipation or nausea — taking iron every other day may work just as well or better than daily dosing.

How long does it take iron supplements to work?

Iron supplementation usually works gradually, not overnight. Some women begin noticing improvements in fatigue, exercise tolerance, or energy within a few weeks, but rebuilding iron stores often takes several months.11 13

How quickly someone improves depends on factors like the severity of deficiency, the type of iron used, how consistently it’s taken, and whether there are underlying absorption issues or ongoing blood losses contributing to the deficiency in the first place.

Can iron supplements cause constipation?

Yes. Constipation is one of the most common side effects of oral iron supplementation, particularly with higher-dose ferrous sulfate products.8 25 Some people also experience nausea, bloating, stomach pain, or dark stools.

Switching to a gentler form like ferrous bisglycinate, lowering the dose, or trying alternate-day dosing may help improve tolerability for some women.3 39

Can you take too much iron?

Yes. Unlike some vitamins, the body has no efficient way to actively eliminate excess iron.24 Taking high-dose iron long term without actually needing it may eventually contribute to iron overload and oxidative stress.

That’s one reason many healthcare providers recommend confirming low iron through blood testing — often including ferritin and hemoglobin — before starting long-term supplementation.34

Is low iron the same thing as anemia?

Not exactly. Iron deficiency develops on a spectrum. Some women have low iron stores (low ferritin) without meeting the criteria for iron-deficiency anemia.13

In those cases, symptoms like fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, brain fog, or hair shedding may still occur even before hemoglobin levels fall low enough to qualify as anemia.13 18
