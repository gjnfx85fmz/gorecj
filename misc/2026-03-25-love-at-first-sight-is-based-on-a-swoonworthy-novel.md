To All the Boys I've Loved Before launched the career of Noah Centineo and spawned two sequels and a spinoff, and now the producers of that movie have just dropped another romantic comedy based on a hugely popular YA novel in collaboration with Netflix.

Love at First Sight might sound like the latest addition to Netflix's ever-growing stable of reality dating shows, but it is actually a sweet movie about missed connections and second chances starring Haley Lu Richardson (The White Lotus) and Ben Hardy (Bohemian Rhapsody). There is also some real comedy pedigree in the supporting cast, which includes Rob Delaney (Catastrophe) and Sally Phillips (Veep).

Richardson plays Hadley Sullivan, a nervous flyer who is traveling from New York to London to attend her father's wedding. While waiting to board the plane, she strikes up a conversation with Yale student Oliver (Hardy), who is originally from the UK. The pair then end up seated next to each other for their transatlantic journey, and the first act of the movie takes place over the seven-hour flight as they get to know each other and, against all odds, begin to wonder if they might be made for each other.

Of course, this is a romcom and so some complications are required, and the plot conspires to separate Hadley and Oliver once they touch down in London. Will they find their way back to each other?

The inspiration for the movie was the 2013 young adult romance novel The Statistical Probability of Love at First Sight by Jennifer E. Williams.

[on Swoonworthy Novel](https://focus-and-calm.sunsimiaosd.com/blog/6015136849.html)

One of the challenges in adapting a book for the screen is how to convey what characters are thinking, often recounted in great detail on the page. Love at First Sight has found an interesting way around that problem. Rounding out the cast is Jameela Jamil (The Good Place) as a flight attendant who breaks the fourth wall and serves as a narrator for Hadley and Oliver's love story. (Jamil's voiceover in the trailer seems to be an intentional nod to Hugh Grant's opening monologue in the 2003 romcom Love, Actually.)

This is a creative choice which appears to be new for the movie, as the novel is told from Hadley's point of view and does not include an omniscient narrator. You'll have to read the book to see if any other details have been tweaked or changed in the adaptation from page to screen.
