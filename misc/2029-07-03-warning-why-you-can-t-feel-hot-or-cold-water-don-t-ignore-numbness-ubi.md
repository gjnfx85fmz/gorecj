The Danger of Numbness: Why You Can't Feel Hot or Cold Water

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Foot numbness that prevents you from feeling hot or cold water is a warning sign of significant nerve damage (peripheral neuropathy). Common causes include diabetes, vitamin deficiencies (such as B12), nerve compression, thyroid disorders, and circulation problems. Without sensation, your feet are vulnerable to undetected burns, frostbite, cuts, and chronic, slow-healing wounds that can lead to serious infection.

Recognizing the underlying cause early is critical. Warning signs, diagnostic tests, and treatment options vary based on the root condition — so identifying yours is the first step toward protecting your feet and overall health.

How Temperature Sensation Works

Our ability to sense heat and cold relies on a complex network:

[Feel Hot](https://jointrevive.xianfengchem.com/blog/2823345794.html)

Thermoreceptors: Specialized nerve endings in the skin detect temperature changes.

Peripheral nerves: Carry signals from these receptors up the legs and into the spinal cord.

Spinal cord and brain: Process signals, interpret them, and trigger appropriate responses (e.g., pulling away from scalding hot water).

Damage or interruption at any point along this pathway can blunt or eliminate temperature perception.

Common Causes of Foot Numbness

Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathy

High blood sugar levels damage small blood vessels and nerves.

Often starts in the toes and progresses upward ("stocking" pattern).

According to the American Diabetes Association, nearly half of people with diabetes develop some form of neuropathy.

Nerve Compression or Entrapment

Conditions like tarsal tunnel syndrome (entrapment of the tibial nerve near the ankle) can block temperature signals.

Herniated discs in the lower back may compress nerve roots supplying the feet.

Vitamin Deficiencies

Lack of B vitamins (especially B12) harms nerve health.

Vegetarians, older adults, and those with absorption issues are at higher risk.

Alcohol-Related Neuropathy

Chronic heavy drinking can poison nerve fibers and deplete essential nutrients.

Autoimmune and Inflammatory Disorders

Conditions like Guillain-Barré syndrome, lupus, or rheumatoid arthritis may target peripheral nerves.

Infections and Toxins

Lyme disease, HIV, and certain toxins (e.g., heavy metals) can trigger nerve damage.

Trauma or Surgery

Direct injury to the foot or ankle can sever or crush nerves responsible for temperature detection.

Recognizing the Warning Signs

If you can't feel temperature in your feet, watch for:

Burning or stabbing pains (especially at night)

Tingling, "pins and needles" or electric shock–like sensations

Muscle weakness or difficulty lifting the front part of your foot (foot drop)

Ulcers, infections, or slow-healing wounds on the feet

Changes in skin color, hair loss, or shiny skin on the feet

Early detection is key. Left unattended, numbness can lead to severe burns, frostbite, or chronic wounds that may require amputation.

Diagnosis: What to Expect

A healthcare provider will likely:

Take a detailed history

Onset, duration, and pattern of numbness

Associated symptoms (pain, weakness, balance issues)

Medical conditions, medications, lifestyle factors

Perform a physical exam

Treatment Strategies

Effective treatment depends on the root cause:

Tight glucose management can slow or halt progression in diabetic neuropathy.

Supplement B vitamins under medical supervision.

Anticonvulsants (e.g., gabapentin) and certain antidepressants can ease neuropathic pain.

Strengthening and balance exercises reduce fall risk and improve nerve health.

Necessary for nerve entrapment syndromes not responding to conservative care.

Limit alcohol intake, adopt a balanced diet, and quit smoking to improve circulation.

Preventing Further Damage

To protect your feet and overall nerve function:

Inspect your feet daily for cuts, blisters, or signs of infection.

Wear comfortable, well-fitting shoes and never walk barefoot on hot or cold surfaces.

Maintain healthy blood sugar levels, even if you're only prediabetic.

Stay active—regular exercise promotes blood flow and nerve health.

Keep alcohol consumption within recommended limits.

When to Seek Immediate Help

Loss of temperature sensation can mask serious injuries. Seek prompt medical attention if you notice:

Unexplained foot ulcers, persistent redness, or swelling

Signs of infection (pus, increased warmth, fever)

Sudden onset of weakness or paralysis in one or both feet

Severe, unrelenting pain

Persistent numbness that interferes with daily activities

Symptoms that worsen over time or spread upward

Any sign of serious infection or injury

Early intervention can prevent irreversible nerve damage and reduce the risk of complications.
