# How 5 Guys Rehabbed Their Hearts After Health Scares

“I Cut My LDL Cholesterol in Half Naturally”

Kolja Sahm, 48

“I consider myself to be healthy and fit. I lift weights and prep for triathlons about 12 to 13 hours per week. However, my LDL eventually climbed to 136 mg/dL. My doctor said I could either go on medication or start eating a plant-based, whole-food diet. I opted for the latter. Within three months, my LDL fell to 87. My doctor was impressed. She said only about 20 percent of patients see dramatic shifts in their cholesterol levels when they change their diets, and I am one of them.”

Tip: “One challenge is getting enough protein, so I’ve learned to eat lots of lentils, seeds, nuts, and soy protein. Another is social pressure: I fell back to my old habits during a holiday and a ski vacation. To stick with this way of eating, I find it helps to communicate that I’m changing my diet for health reasons. People seem to respect that.”

I felt a pain in my chest and left elbow and pulled over to ask a bystander for help. He drove me to a water station I’d just passed, where I collapsed. After being loaded into an ambulance, I went into cardiac arrest and was shocked nine times while on the way to the hospital.

“The doctor implanted a tiny heart pump called the Impella that allowed my heart to rest. Then I got a stent. I went home two weeks later, I was determined to recover and finish the ride I’d started. At first, I could only manage a few steps with a walker and a nurse’s help. But I was back on my bike in three months and in April completed the last 10 miles of the ride.”

Tip: “Keep moving. My cardiologist attributes my recovery to my physical conditioning before my heart attack. But I didn’t rest on the past. I also lost 15 pounds through cardiac rehab, physical therapy, and with a heart-healthy diet that’s mostly vegetarian. I am lighter, stronger, and more flexible than before.”

“I Listened and Learned in a Support Group"

Chris Schornak, 55

“After I had a heart attack, I realized how little I knew about my heart. I smoked and was proud of how much bacon I could eat. Shortly after, I stopped smoking, and I now eat more salmon and vegetables. I also noticed that the medical system is good at repairing our hearts, but it struggles to address the emotional and psychological aspects of heart disease. So I started a patient-led heart attack support group at Henry Ford Macomb Cardiac Rehabilitation. We talk about what we’re going through and share information.”

“I Stopped Putting Things Off"

Dave Conway, 37

“I was a 30-year-old distiller when I started feeling dizzy, sweaty, breathless, and lethargic. I thought I had heat exhaustion from rolling a 400-pound bourbon barrel. Or maybe it was aging. After three weeks, my manager asked if I needed to see a doctor. I dragged myself to urgent care, annoyed to be there on a Sunday night. They sent me straight to the hospital, which irked me more because I was sure it was no big deal. But it was: I’d unknowingly survived a widow-maker heart attack.

“I stopped drinking and smoking. I stopped ordering pizza at 2 a.m., and I started therapy. I did cardiac rehab at the Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center.”

Tip: “I used to put off healthy habits, thinking, That’s tomorrow Dave’s problem, instead of doing the right thing in the moment. Now, when something’s off, I’m willing to ask a doctor for help. If there’s something wrong, you can address it before it causes permanent damage.”

“Playing the Trumpet Boosted My Cardio Fitness.”

Kevin Wilson, 66

“Before my quadruple bypass surgery, my surgeon told me I was the healthiest sickest guy he had ever operated on. I play tennis, work out six days a week, and go to the doctor every year. I never had high cholesterol or any other signs of trouble. The doctors think it was genetic. Now, I do things that offer insurance against another heart attack. My kids got me an Apple Watch, which I wear when I play tennis so I can pause if my heart rate goes over the recommended rate. And I stay disciplined about recovery.”

Tip: I was really bothered by the breathing capacity I lost. At first, I kept working at blowing into a spirometer, a small device to measure lung function. Later, I returned to a hobby I love: I started practicing my trumpet with soft, easy notes. After about three months, I started playing concerts with my orchestra again. I can play as well as before–it actually feels better now. A strong heart and strong lungs work together.”
