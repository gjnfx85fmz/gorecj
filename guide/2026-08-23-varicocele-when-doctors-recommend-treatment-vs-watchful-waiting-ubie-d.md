Explanation

A varicocele is an enlargement of the veins within the scrotum, similar to varicose veins that can occur in the legs. It affects up to 15% of adult men and is most common between ages 15 and 25. While many men experience no symptoms, others may face discomfort, fertility concerns, or testicular changes. Deciding between watchful waiting and active treatment depends on individual symptoms, goals, and risk factors.

What Is a Varicocele?

Veins in the scrotum (the pampiniform plexus) become dilated due to faulty valves.

Leads to blood pooling, increased testicular temperature, and potential pressure on the testicle.

Often occurs on the left side (80–90% of cases) but can be bilateral.

Common Signs and Symptoms

Most varicoceles are discovered incidentally during a physical exam. When symptoms do occur, they may include:

A dull, aching pain or heaviness in the scrotum, especially after standing or physical activity

Noticeable enlargement or "bag of worms" feeling in the scrotum

Testicular atrophy (shrinking of the affected testicle)

Subfertility or infertility concerns (seen on semen analysis)

Visible or palpable veins above the testicle

How Is a Varicocele Diagnosed?

Physical Examination

Performed while standing and during a Valsalva maneuver (bearing down).

A doctor may feel the dilated veins above the testicle.

Scrotal Ultrasound

Confirms diagnosis, measures vein diameter, and assesses blood flow.

When to Choose Watchful Waiting

Many varicoceles remain stable without causing significant problems. Doctors often recommend observation when:

You have minimal or no discomfort.

There's no evidence of testicular atrophy.

Fertility is not currently a concern (normal semen analysis).

You're not planning to conceive in the near future.

Symptoms don't worsen over time.

Benefits of Watchful Waiting

Avoids potential surgical or procedural risks.

No recovery downtime.

Regular monitoring ensures changes are detected early.

What Monitoring Looks Like

Annual physical exams to check for changes in size or symptoms.

Repeat ultrasound if new symptoms (pain, swelling) appear.

Follow-up semen analysis if fertility becomes a concern.

When Treatment Is Recommended

Active treatment is typically advised if one or more of the following apply:

Chronic Pain or Discomfort

Persistent aching that interferes with daily life or physical activity.

Testicular Atrophy

Noticeable shrinkage of the affected testicle, which may affect hormone production.

Infertility Concerns

Abnormal semen parameters linked to a varicocele.

Couples having difficulty conceiving for 12 months or more.

Cosmetic or Psychological Distress

Significant enlargement bothers the patient.

High-Grade Varicocele

Large, easily noticeable varicocele (Grade II–III) more likely to cause problems.

Treatment Options

Microsurgical Varicocelectomy

Most common surgical approach.

Uses an operating microscope to ligate (tie off) dilated veins.

High success rate (>90%) with low recurrence and complication rates.

Outpatient procedure; return to light activity in 1–2 days, full recovery in 2–4 weeks.

Laparoscopic Varicocelectomy

Small incisions in the abdomen; a camera guides vein ligation.

Similar success to microsurgery but may involve general anesthesia and slightly longer recovery.

Percutaneous Embolization

Interventional radiology procedure.

A catheter is threaded into the vein, and coils or a sclerosing agent block blood flow.

Minimally invasive, same-day discharge.

Success rates around 80–85%, with a small risk of recurrence.

Possible Complications

Hydrocele (fluid buildup around testicle)

Infection or bleeding

Recurrence of varicocele (5–15% of cases)

Rare injury to surrounding structures

Expected Outcomes

Pain relief in over 90% of treated cases.

Improved semen quality in 60–70% of men treated for infertility.

Pregnancy rates increase by up to 40% in couples where varicocele repair addressed male-factor infertility.

Self-Care and Lifestyle Measures

While waiting or recovering from treatment, men can:

Wear supportive underwear or an athletic supporter to relieve discomfort.

Apply cold packs to the scrotum for 10–15 minutes several times daily.

Avoid heavy lifting or strenuous exercise until cleared by a doctor.

Maintain a healthy weight and avoid smoking, both of which can impact vascular health.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Help

Contact a healthcare provider if you experience:

Sudden, severe scrotal pain or swelling.

Fever over 100.4°F (38°C).

Signs of infection (redness, warmth, discharge).

Any concerning changes in testicle size or consistency.

These symptoms could indicate testicular torsion, infection, or other urgent conditions.

Making Your Decision

Choosing between watchful waiting and active treatment depends on:

Severity of discomfort.

Fertility goals and semen analysis results.

Testicular size changes.

Personal preferences and lifestyle.

Discuss with your urologist or primary care doctor to weigh benefits, risks, and timing.
